Special relativity, from the arithmetic up

Everything you think you know about speed is slightly wrong.

Not in the details — in the parts that matter. Einstein didn't make the universe stranger. He made it stranger in one specific, describable way, and then the strangeness turned out to be testable to extraordinary precision. This page builds that one idea from the ground up, using arithmetic you could check on a calculator.

Who and where, for the next six modules

You

On a small ship called the Wayfarer, with a clock bolted to the wall. You are the one moving, and you are the one who will come back.

Ana

Your friend, standing on Earth in the departure hangar with the same kind of clock. She never boards. She is not the slow one and she is not the fast one — she is the control.

The destination

Proxima Centauri, 4.25 light-years out — the nearest star. Every distance on this page is measured from Earth's hangar door.

Two clocks, one journey. The whole of relativity is the disagreement between what those two clocks say when you come back.

Module 1

The one number that changes everything

All of it hangs off a single quantity: how fast you're going, compared to light.

That ratio is called β, and the number that matters is γ, which is just a rearrangement of Pythagoras:

Here is the whole setup. You are aboard a small ship, the Wayfarer, accelerating out of Earth's hangar on the way to Proxima Centauri. Your friend Ana is standing on the tarmac watching you go. You each have a good clock. The speed slider below sets how fast you're going.

The thing worth noticing in that formula is not its strangeness but its shape. It is flat until you're moving fast, and then it runs away without limit. There is no regime where it becomes more strange; it just stops being negligible.

Why that shape? Because space and time mix. If you measure how long something is and how long it takes, and you insist that light goes the same speed no matter who's measuring, the arithmetic that falls out is exactly the expression above. Light isn't special because it's fast. It's special because it's the same speed for everyone, and that one fact forces the trade.

Two observers can look at the same clock and honestly disagree about how long it took. That sounds like the setup to a cheat, so it's worth being precise about what they do agree on.

They agree on the ticks. Both count the same number of bounces of the light beam between the mirrors. They disagree about how much of their own time passed while that happened. Since every experiment they can possibly run has to go through light, there is no experiment that settles the disagreement — both of them are doing correct physics.

So the practical upshot of module 1 is a clock, moving, and a rate. That's the whole mechanism. Everything after this is a consequence — and all of it concerns the same two people and the same trip.

Two things this is not. It isn't a theory about gravity — that's general relativity, a different and much harder subject. And it isn't optional: GPS satellites carry clocks that run about seven microseconds fast per day from motion alone. Switch the correction off and your phone's map drifts by roughly ten kilometres a year.

It is not the distance

Almost everyone's first guess is that time runs slow out in space because space is a long way away — as though empty space were a thick medium you had to push through. It isn't, and this is the single misconception worth killing on the first day.

If you teleported to Proxima and sat perfectly still, your clock would tick at exactly the normal rate. Sit still four light-years from anywhere and you are no slower than anyone. Distance, on its own, does nothing at all.

What makes clocks disagree is motion and gravity — and those are two separate effects that both get called "time dilation." Gravity is the tiny one here, and it belongs to general relativity, which this page is not about. Its strength depends on how much mass is nearby, not on how far you have travelled. At interplanetary speeds it is roughly ten billion times weaker than the effect of simply moving.

The size of the gravity effect, for calibration. GPS satellites 20,000 km up gain about 46 microseconds a day from weaker gravity and lose about 7 from moving fast, netting roughly 38 microseconds fast. That is the whole effect, and it matters because a navigation fix needs nanosecond accuracy. At 99% of light speed your clock runs seven times slower — a factor of seven, not a fraction of a microsecond.

And in module 4 you'll see that even the traveller's age difference is not about the 4.25 light-years. The distance is scenery. What costs them time is turning around.

Module 2

What it costs you

Nobody is moved by a Lorentz factor. People are moved by asking what the number does to a life.

Pick a destination and a speed below. The bar is to scale: your clock against Earth's. The ratio between them is γ, exactly, with no fudge factor.

The comparison that usually lands is the last line. At high enough speed you get home before a light signal sent from Earth today would arrive back — even though you took a path out and back that light needed years to cover. You simply experienced less time doing it.

The catch is the last clause of the sentence, and it's the whole catch: everyone at home lived through that entire stretch of years, and you were not there for any of it.

Module 3

"Right now" is not a fact

Here's the part that feels like a trick, and isn't. Two events that happen at the same instant for you happen at different instants for someone moving relative to you.

To measure how long a moving object is, you have to note where its two ends are at the same moment in your frame. That's the definition of length — you're comparing two positions at one time. Pick a different moment and you get a different number.

Which moment should you pick? Here's the trap: it feels as though there is a universal now, a background clock the whole universe keeps, and that you could in principle consult it. There isn't. Each observer slices spacetime at their own angle, and the slices disagree.

Length contraction falls out of this immediately. The rod measures itself with its own simultaneous events and gets its rest length. You measure it with yours and get something shorter. Neither of you is wrong, because "at the same time" never pointed at the same pair of events in both frames.

And it's the same mechanism as module 1, aimed somewhere else. If a moving rod is also being used as a clock — a length that ticks — then finding that fewer of its seconds fit per unit of length means each second takes longer to pass. Same geometry, same γ.

This is why the two modules agree. Nobody has ever measured a moving object's length the naive way and got a longer answer. They measure it the way length has to be measured — simultaneously, in their own frame — and they get a shorter one. The surprise is only ever in the naive expectation.
Module 4

The traveller comes home older

Put the previous idea on a round trip and the most famous argument in relativity resolves cleanly.

Two twins. Ana stays on Earth. You leave for Proxima Centauri, turn around, come home. You meet. One of you is older. Which one, and why doesn't that break anything?

Start with the part everyone agrees on: you both left at the same moment, you both got back at the same moment, and the clocks at the reunion show the difference. No disagreement there.

The picture that resolves it is a diagram of spacetime rather than of space. Vertical is time, horizontal is space, and each person's life is the line they trace through it.

The time you feel is the length of your own path between departure and reunion. Ana's path is perfectly straight — she never changes course. Yours dives out into space and comes back. And in the geometry of spacetime, a straight path is the longest one, not the shortest. Bending into space costs you time.

This is the opposite of ordinary intuition. In a map, the straight line between two places is the shortest route. In spacetime it's the one that buys you the most time. Anyone who finds that suspicious is in good company: it took the better part of a century to be taken seriously.
Module 5

The wall, not the goalpost

Why light speed can't be passed — two independent reasons, and you need both.

The first is geometric. If two things are moving, you don't add their speeds — you add them the way you add angles. Which means two objects each doing ninety percent of light speed don't jointly do one hundred and eighty. They jointly do slightly under one hundred.

The second is the one that actually stops rockets, because velocity addition on its own isn't a rule anything is forced to obey. The energy curve is. Newtonian kinetic energy stays finite at any speed, so Newton had no reason to object to going faster and faster. Relativistic energy climbs without limit as you approach light, so there is always another push needed and never a finite push that finishes the job.

Module 6

Mass is energy, already

E = mc² is usually shown as a curiosity. Here's the version that's worth remembering.

That energy exists whether or not anything is moving. It isn't created by speed — speed adds to a quantity that was already there. The rest energy is sitting in the mass, and the number attached to it is absurdly large.

The reason it stays that way is that converting mass to energy is ruinously difficult. Annihilation converts all of it at once and requires meeting something moving at nearly light speed. Splitting nuclei releases a fraction of a percent. Which is why a kilogram of matter is worth twenty megatons of TNT and also completely unobtainable as a power source.

Every number on this page — γ, the clock rates, the trip durations, the energy figures, the velocity sums — is computed at runtime by a single small TypeScript module, not hard-coded. The same module that does the arithmetic is checked by thirty-eight assertions covering the textbook values (γ at 0.6c, γ at 0.8c, E=mc², the energy–momentum identity) and the boundary conditions where the formulas stop applying.

This is deliberately only special relativity: flat spacetime, inertial frames, no gravity. General relativity — curved spacetime, black holes, the expansion of the universe — is a different and much larger subject, and it agrees with everything here wherever they overlap.
